Normalised difference vegetation index (NDVI)

Normalised difference vegetation index (NDVI)

The normalised difference vegetation index (NDVI) is the most used number in remote sensing of vegetation. It compares how much red light and how much near-infrared light a surface reflects:

NDVI = (NIR − Red) / (NIR + Red)

The result lies between −1 and 1. Green vegetation gives high values, bare soil and built surfaces values near 0, and water negative values. The index was introduced in the 1970s for the early Landsat satellites (Rouse et al., 1974; Tucker, 1979). It has been calculated for the whole Earth almost every day since the 1980s, which makes it one of the longest records of the state of the planet’s vegetation.

Why it works: chlorophyll and photosynthesis

NDVI measures the contrast that photosynthesis creates. Chlorophyll absorbs red and blue light to drive photosynthesis, and the leaf’s inner structure reflects near-infrared light, which it cannot use:

Top: how much light chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b and carotenoids absorb at each wavelength. Bottom: the rate of photosynthesis at each wavelength. Figure: John Whitmarsh and Govindjee, CC BY-SA 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons.

The figure shows the two absorption peaks of chlorophyll, in blue (about 430–480 nm) and red (about 640–680 nm), and the low absorption in green between them. Photosynthesis follows the absorption: it is fastest in blue and red light, slower in green, and drops to almost nothing above 700 nm. The red band used for NDVI lies on chlorophyll a’s red peak; the near-infrared band lies beyond 700 nm, where nothing is absorbed. The more green, healthy leaves there are in a pixel, the darker it is in red and the brighter in near infrared, and the higher the NDVI.

Using a normalised difference rather than the bands themselves has two advantages. The index has a fixed range, so values from different places and dates can be compared. And because it is a ratio, much of what affects both bands equally cancels out: differences in illumination from slope or sun angle, and the scale factor with which reflectance is stored.

The band numbers differ between sensors, and so do the exact wavelengths, so NDVI from two sensors is close but not identical. Compare values from the same sensor, or harmonise them first.

A year of NDVI in Denmark

NDVI over Denmark and southern Sweden in 2022, one frame per 16 days, from light brown (low NDVI) to dark green (high NDVI). Water is masked in black. Animation: Esbern Holmes, calculated in Google Earth Engine (script).

The animation shows the phenology of the landscape, the yearly rhythm of the vegetation:

  • Winter (January–February): NDVI is low, but Denmark is not brown. Grass, winter cereals and winter rape stay green through the mild winter. In southern Sweden the lowest values of the year come in mid-January, most likely from snow.
  • Spring (March–May): the fields green up and the deciduous forests come into leaf. NDVI rises fast through April and May.
  • Early summer (late May–June): the whole landscape reaches its maximum. Fields, forests and grassland look almost alike: NDVI has saturated (see below).
  • Late summer (late July–August): Denmark turns lighter and patchy while the Swedish forests stay dark. The cereals ripen and are harvested, and the fields go from green to yellow to stubble. Most of Denmark is farmland, most of southern Sweden forest, and NDVI shows the difference without any map of land use.
  • Autumn (September–November): new growth on harvested fields, winter crops coming up and grass keep NDVI moderate until the leaves fall and the light fades.

Two technical points lie behind such an animation:

  • Compositing. Clouds reflect red and near infrared about equally, so cloudy pixels have an NDVI near 0. A single image of Denmark is rarely cloud-free. Each frame is therefore a composite of all images within a period, here 16 days, keeping for each pixel a clear observation, often the one with the highest NDVI (the maximum value composite). The price is that a frame is not a single date, and the value of a pixel may come from any day in the period.
  • Scale. At the scale of a country the pixels are large, and each frame shows regions rather than fields. At 10 m (Sentinel-2) the same rhythm can be followed field by field, see Vegetation greenness at Ledreborg (Example).

What NDVI measures, and what it does not

NDVI measures greenness: how much green, chlorophyll-bearing leaf area there is in a pixel. It is closely related to quantities like leaf area, the fraction of light absorbed by plants, and photosynthesis, but it is none of them:

  • Saturation. Above an NDVI of about 0.8, more leaves hardly change the value. Dense forests and closed crops look alike, as in the June frames of the animation. Red-edge indices and the enhanced vegetation index (EVI) stay sensitive longer.
  • Soil and background. In sparse vegetation the soil shows through, and its colour and moisture change the value.
  • Green is not healthy, and not productive. A lawn and a forest can have the same NDVI. Water stress may show only after some delay, when the leaves have already lost chlorophyll.
  • Atmosphere. Haze adds most to the red band and lowers NDVI. Use surface reflectance (see top-of-atmosphere and surface reflectance), and mask clouds and their shadows.
  • Time. NDVI follows the season. Two images are only comparable if they are from the same time of year, as the animation shows; a change between April and June is not a change in land use.
