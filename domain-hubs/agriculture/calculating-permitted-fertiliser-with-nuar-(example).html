Calculating permitted fertiliser with NUAR (Example)

Calculating permitted fertiliser with NUAR (Example)

Properties1
tags—

Nov 26, 20267 min read

A worked case for the agriculture hub, on Denmark’s discharge-based area regulation (Udledningsbaseret Arealregulering, UMR) entering force in 2027. Code: https://github.com/Esbern/nuar.

From 2027 a field’s permitted mineral fertiliser is tied to how much nitrate it is modelled to leach from the root zone, and how much of that leaching is retained before it reaches the coast. The calculation runs through NLES5, Aarhus University’s statistical leaching model, as implemented in SEGES’s reference “NUAR-regneark” (Excel, v1.3) — an authoritative but partly provisional tool: SEGES’s own documentation states outright that several of its inputs (soil data, the runoff map) are not yet finalised. This example works one point through the full chain: crop and soil → leaching → retention → discharge, and treats the retention step on purpose as a modelling question, not a lookup.

1. Spatial Intent

Question: given a field’s crop, soil and climate, and the nitrogen retention between it and the coast, how much mineral fertiliser N can be applied without the field’s net discharge exceeding its 2027 quota?

Theory: NLES5 is a regression model fitted to suction-cup leaching measurements; it estimates root-zone leaching (kg N/ha) from crop, cover, soil texture, climate and fertilisation. What actually reaches the coast is leaching × (1 − retention) — and retention, as published, is not a property of the field but of the catchment it drains through on the way to a named recipient. That distinction is the point of this example.

Known limits: the soil input is a 56-bin simplification of what Aarhus University’s own newer soil mapping treats as a continuous clay-content surface. The runoff/percolation lookup (AfstrDat) has the largest single effect on the result of anything tested (~17% of leaching variance in sensitivity runs, against ~1% for climate-grid/region) and was, as of SEGES’s 16.06.2026 note, still “under udvikling” at Aarhus University. See Soil for the broader question of modelled vs. surveyed soil data in Denmark.

3. Retention is a hydrological model output, not a field property

The retention layer looks, at first glance, like any other lookup: join a point to a polygon, read off a percentage. Two things about its schema argue against treating it that way.

First, every polygon carries a KystvandID — the id of the specific coastal water body the retention value is accounted toward. The number published is not “the fraction of this field’s nitrogen lost in transit,” independent of where it ends up; it is already attributed to one recipient. A field at the edge of two catchments draining to different coastal waters will see different retention even if the land between it and the coast looks identical, because the two numbers are computed against two different recipients, not two different transport distances.

Second, a retention percentage of this kind is normally derived from a hydrological model — flow paths, travel time, subsurface denitrification potential — run against some reference loading, not measured per field. That makes it a direct output of the same kind of model this vault’s Hydrological modelling hub covers, with the same caveats: sensitive to how the underlying terrain/flow model was built, and to assumptions baked in at calibration time rather than stated on the data layer itself. Some retention maps of this type in Denmark show a 10 km homogeneity in places — a resolution that would be unremarkable for a national groundwater flow model (such as DK-model/HIP) but is unexpected for something published as a field-relevant agricultural layer. Whether that is in fact inherited from reuse of a coarser hydrological model’s own grid, rather than being resolved at the retention calculation itself, is an open research question, not yet confirmed from this layer’s own metadata.

Practical consequence: retention_pct should be read, and taught, as “this catchment’s modelled retention toward this specific recipient, under the assumptions of whatever hydrological model produced it” — not as a fixed physical constant of the land between a field and the sea.

The reference is NUAR’s own built-in comparison case (spring barley with no measures), so 101% reads as “about what an unremarkable spring-barley field is expected to leach” — the retention step, not the leaching estimate, is doing almost all of the work in bringing discharge down to 0.28 kg N/ha at this particular, very high-retention point.

6. Points for the discussion

  • Retention is recipient-conditioned, not a transport coefficient (§3). Before using RetTot across fields draining to different coastal waters, check whether it is meant to be comparable at all, or whether it is only valid relative to each polygon’s own named recipient.
  • A 10 km artefact, possibly inherited. Where the retention layer shows unexpectedly coarse, grid-like homogeneity, the likely explanation is reuse of a national hydrological model’s own resolution (HIP/DK-model) rather than a resolution chosen for retention itself — worth confirming against the layer’s methodology documentation, which is not published alongside the WFS service.
  • Soil input is categorical by construction, not by necessity. NUAR discretises soil into 56 (region × JB-group × subsoil) bins; Aarhus University’s own newer soil maps are built from a continuous clay-content surface. SEGES’s 11.05.2026 note states plainly that whether soil type “documented via jordprøver” can enter the regulation at all is still undecided.
  • The highest-leverage input was, by SEGES’s own account, still being built. AfstrDat (runoff/percolation) affects the result roughly 17× more than the climate grid does, and was “under udvikling” as of June 2026. landbrugsgeodata.fvm.dk now lists an Afstroemningsvaerdier_2027 layer that may be its finished replacement — not yet checked against the workbook’s own lookup.
  • The climate grid id is a reconstruction. NUAR’s legacy 10001–10609 numbering of DMI’s 10 km cells is not published anywhere; the crosswalk used here is strongly evidenced (an exact 18-cell match against the workbook’s own picture, the isolated Bornholm cluster landing correctly) but should be flagged as inferred whenever it is relied on.
